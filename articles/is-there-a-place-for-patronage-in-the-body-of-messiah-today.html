The Bible and the Artist

Is There a Place for Patronage in the Body of Messiah Today?

2016 · The Bible and the Artist

A dialogue between a Ukrainian businesswoman and an Israeli artist on patronage and support for creative projects.

Natali Osypa graduated from the European University, Faculty of Finance and Entrepreneurship, and is the author of the "Christian Business" project.

Ephraim Kalish: Is there a place for patronage in the Body of Messiah today? I have wanted to talk with businesspeople about this for a long time. It is not a comfortable subject, since it may be taken to mean that I want to get money and so on.

Financial support for an artist is of course always good, but what concerns me is the very heart of the problem. It is no secret that an artist's life is not easy. And the chance to live from one's art looks to many like a fairy tale… The tasks and problems facing creative people exist everywhere, but here in Israel they are multiplied tenfold by the nature of things. Remember the joke: if a Jew comes down the steps of a plane without a violin in his hands, it means he plays the piano. Being a musician in Israel is very hard: the country is small and there are very many musicians. You cannot set up a symphony orchestra for every city block… And they keep on arriving. In the foreseeable future this process will only grow. After all, God promised to gather all the Jews from all the countries where He scattered them. Hollywood will pick itself up and move… I believe that creative Israelis have a special calling to serve the nations in the last days. Hence my strong interest in how to support a creative person. I am in touch with creative people here in Israel. Many are simply lost, depressed and so on.

Natali Osypa: Here the very term "patronage" has taken on a colouring of begging for money. Yet it is simply investment in art, and that has always paid off. Besides, historically patrons have often had the role of inspirers and direct participants in the works of God alongside apostles and prophets. For example, the first Russian printer Ivan Fyodorov and the magnate Alexander Khodkevich who gave him refuge; Martin Luther and his protector, the Elector Frederick of Saxony; and there were even women who "ministered unto him of their substance" to Jesus (Luke 8:3). Patrons share the credit with God's called people both for spreading the Gospel and for many turns in world history. I believe businesspeople in the Church need to be taught this view of patronage: "He that receiveth a prophet in the name of a prophet shall receive a prophet's reward" (Matt. 10:41).

But there is another side: creative people themselves. Few of them are aware of their place and prophetic role in the Church. Because "lost and depressed" is not the biblical way. And an artist receives according to faith, just like everyone else.

Ephraim Kalish: Hmm. Everything you say is right. The same could be said of drug addicts: that is not the biblical way either. Yet believers understand that they must be helped and set on the way of truth. And then, once strengthened, they will be able to serve and fulfil the calling given them by the Most High… In a certain sense an artist is also an "addict". I call the creative fraternity a "risk group".

Natali Osypa: True. This problem has to be solved from both sides: on the one hand, preaching to business about God's will for art, and on the other, guiding creative people. Because if an artist inspired by God brings his idea to a businessperson with faith, the chances of being refused fall sharply.

Ephraim Kalish: And it seems to me that here we must speak of the community of believers as a body in which there are different members caring for one another. As for the gifts God gives each of us, they are given so that we serve one another and the community, and in this way God. I am not talking about someone doing someone else a favour, but about each doing his part of the work and of the commission from the Most High: artists their service, businesspeople theirs. In this there would be harmony and God's order.

Natali Osypa: Yes. That is exactly how it works.

Ephraim Kalish: You see, you and I have already come to an agreement.

Natali Osypa: And the matter does not necessarily lie on the level of money at all. We need to learn how the anointings that rest on each of us work together: whether it is an anointing for organising a business, for creating artistic images, for preaching and so on. If, doing what we are called to (and therefore know how to do), we help our neighbour with our anointing (advice, connections, knowledge and so on), that works even better than simply "giving money".

Ephraim Kalish: I fully agree: you can give a fish, or you can help and teach someone to fish.

Natali Osypa: That is exactly the example I was thinking of. But people as a rule simply do not see these options. When you explain the options to them and ask businesspeople more concrete questions, they are much more willing to take part.

And in the Church too the role of creativity needs to be explained, both for preaching the Gospel and for the life of the community. From my own practice I can say that art stands somewhere alongside praying in tongues. When an idea (any idea: business, scientific, social) passes from the spirit into the material world, people of art are the first to sense it and express it in images. What the rational mind cannot yet "grasp" may already exist in this world in the form of artistic images. Then businesspeople, scientists, public figures and prophets take these images apart for their own work. We call this inspiration. (I have just thought: how many prophets in congregations have been left without inspiration because there was no artist beside them at the right moment. Remember Elisha, who needed a minstrel? "But now bring me a minstrel. And it came to pass, when the minstrel played, that the hand of the LORD came upon him" (2 Kings 3:15).) Business needs to have the role of creativity in social and economic processes explained to it, and artists too need to have their role explained to them.

It is a general problem of the Church: the attitude to promoting others. Recently I found an article in English, and here is my translation.

Ephraim Kalish: I think the Creator's plans include the intention to put this situation right.

Natali Osypa: Of course. One can conclude that from the very fact that you are here and have such a calling, and I am here. When I started the "Christian Business" project at the end of 2010, I understood it was a fairly new approach. Later I looked for similar resources and found several sites in English. Interestingly, they had been started at about the same time, give or take a couple of months. So God has clearly begun this movement in the Body of Christ.

It is extremely important to God to win back the sphere of art for the sake of the Gospel and the education of people. Artistic images are taken in better than theories. In the Bible this principle sounds like "I will open my mouth in a parable" (Ps. 78:2). And now we see how "this world" makes full use of this ease with which artistic images are absorbed.

Ephraim Kalish: Interesting! If you write an article on your vision of the coming interaction between Christian business and its own creative potential, I think it will be interesting and important. Thank you.
