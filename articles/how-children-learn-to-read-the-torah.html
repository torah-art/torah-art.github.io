How Children Learn to Read the Torah

2015 · The Bible and the Artist, Biblical Feasts

Victoria Hanna: how children learn to read.

On the feast a rich table is laid, on which there is always food made of milk (cheese, sour cream, curd cheese) and of flour (pies, pancakes, cakes). Dairy food is eaten because the Jews who returned to the camp with the Torah made do with milk products. The dairy is eaten first, and then the meat is served in separate dishes. Sweet things are eaten, especially those made with honey; sweet challahs are baked and served at the festive table.

On the eve of Shavuot the graduates of religious schools receive their certificates. On the day of the giving of the Torah little children begin to be taught Torah. Early in the morning they are taken to the synagogue. A board is set up there on which the letters of the Hebrew alphabet and sentences in Hebrew are written. The teacher reads the letters, and the pupils repeat after him. The board is smeared with honey, and the pupils lick the honey off the letters. Words of the Torah are written on honey cakes and on hard-boiled, shelled eggs (also smeared with honey).

According to tradition, three-year-old Shneur Zalman was carried into the classroom wrapped in a tallit. He licked the honey off the letters of the alphabet so as to feel the sweetness of learning.

Unfortunately, I am unable to show here scans of the Jewish counterpart of the game from the previous post: only family stories and scattered mentions have brought me information about it. According to these stories and mentions, at the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries there were type-boards made for religious Jewish education: Hebrew letters were cut into the wood as hollows of the right shape, forming various commandments and key phrases from the Torah. Honey was poured into the letters, that is, into the hollows. The diligent child was supposed to run a finger along the hollows and, while studying the shape and meaning of the saying, lick the honey off the finger at the same time. In this way several tasks were solved at once: 1) the striving for the fleshly, that is, honey, was cunningly tied to the study of Scripture, since only by moving from letter to letter and from phrase to phrase could the honey be got and eaten to the end; 2) not only with the eyes but, so to speak, by touch itself and with the fingertips, the child memorised the shapes of the letters and the commandments themselves; 3) at the most basic level of conditioned reflexes, one might say, the child came to know all the sweetness of the Torah, for its sayings were joined by association with the taste of honey, and the honey was directly contained in them (the sayings).

Afterwards this procedure could be repeated, but now on a condition: only after reading the commandment aloud and showing that he understood its meaning could the child again get and lick, in the same manner, the honey that had again been thoughtfully poured into the letters.

Apparently only a certain shortage of honey could have hindered the effectiveness of the whole idea, deadly for those of little faith, and led to that irresistible departure from the faith of the fathers which seized the broad Jewish masses of the Russian Empire at the turn of the 19th and 20th centuries.

My mother says that now at Hanukkah they bake biscuits in the shape of the letters of the alphabet. So the old traditions are not forgotten. :)

** There used to be a custom of beginning to teach children Torah precisely on Shavuot. Early in the morning the boys were brought to the synagogue. There the teacher gave them a tablet on which the letters of the Hebrew alphabet were written. The teacher read each letter, and the child repeated after him. The teacher put a little honey on the tablet, and the child licked the honey off the letters.
