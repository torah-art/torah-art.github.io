Art Midrash

The Exodus Is About Us

2018 · Art Midrash, The Bible and the Artist, Biblical Feasts

For us, people of today, it is hard to imagine what it was like: first to live in slavery to the Egyptians, and then to go out into the wilderness and become its captive for forty years. But an artist can.

Whatever an artist does, whether he paints pictures, sings in opera or plays the double bass, he creates his most unique works, the ones closest to other people's hearts, without being aware of it. Unexpectedly, someone with no knowledge of art suddenly understands what troubled the artist or what made him happy at the moment he was creating. Without knowing the story the artist took as his basis, you suddenly begin to understand what came before and after that moment. And it seems you do not know this master personally, but it is as if your souls met somewhere in his work and embraced for a moment.

Oleksandr Antonyuk from the city of Khmelnytskyi is called an artist-philosopher. His creative manner is recognised as distinctive, close to icon painting, with elements of the naive. But that is all the professional, art-historical side. Technique, conformity to rules, classification into categories, the ability to fit within the limits of a genre: all that can be acquired. The professional eye often holds one back and does not let one see that special something that makes a work speak to hearts and souls.

When I saw Oleksandr's works, his paintings clearly "spoke" to me. And this "conversation" was very sincere and rich in information, emotions and very personal associations. Pesach is coming soon, so I will share what I "heard" in the canvas "Exodus".

One of the dramatic events of Jewish history, thanks to the commandment to keep this feast throughout the generations, has reminded everyone living for more than 3,000 years of the events that took place at the meeting point of continents and civilisations.

For us, people of today, it is hard to imagine what it was like: first to live in slavery to the Egyptians, and then to go out into the wilderness and become its captive for forty years. But an artist can. He does not need to know what colour the clothes of people in those ancient times were, how long their hair or beards were, what shade their skin had. What he knows for certain is that they lived on the earth, in a place that lies between the spiritual worlds: the heavens and the underworld. And he is sure why they went out of Egypt: to become once again the people of the living God.

When the Israelites had only just gone beyond the borders of the Egyptian dominions, they carried much of Egypt with them: behaviour, thinking, even preferences in food. Remember how they complained to Moshe: "We remember the fish, which we did eat in Egypt freely; the cucumbers, and the melons, and the leeks, and the onions, and the garlick" (Num. 11:5). I think that is exactly why the artist portrayed them as outwardly very like the Egyptians. This inner state changes with time: the further they go, the more they are transformed into people of a completely different order and cease to look like Egyptian slaves.

The Hebrew text tells that the Israelites went out of Egypt in order to offer sacrifice to the Most High (Exod. 8:27). And the artist shows symbolically that the people came out of darkness and strive towards the light. Those walking on the right, the ones who look like Egyptians, are hidden in darkness. But the further they go, the more they are immersed in light. Those shown on the left practically dissolve in this non-physical light. For they are going to offer sacrifice to the Father of lights, as it is written in the Letter of James (James 1:17).

The Exodus is shown in a line across the centre of the picture as a narrow band cutting the canvas into three parts, and in this it recalls a wall fresco. For me it is a symbol of memory that has not died, of a great history that archaeologists today gather bit by bit to picture the world, gone forever, in which our forefathers lived.

In the upper part of the picture Hebrew letters float: shin, mem, vav and tav, "Shemot", which means "names". Whose names? The names of the Most High? Tzevaot, the God of hosts; Immanuel, God with us; Adonai Yireh, the LORD who sees; Adonai Rofe, the LORD the Healer... Or perhaps the names of those who did not live to see this exodus, who were killed by hard labour, hunger and pain? The memory of them will always be a sign that God did not create us as slaves, that slavery cannot be accepted. Or perhaps the names of those who believed the Most High and went out of Egypt? Perhaps their names are written there, by the throne of God, in the book of the living and the dead?

We know from the Passover Haggadah that only those went out who kept the Pesach at the word of the Lord: who sacrificed a lamb and smeared its blood on the doorposts so that the angel of death would pass by. The one who went out was the one who ate the meat of the lamb with bitter herbs and unleavened bread. The one who went out was the one who, overcoming his fear, took a step into the unknown. These people are the heroes of the Exodus.

Under the feet of the walkers the artist has thrown only grey strokes. They are like the mud or clay the Israelites left behind in Egypt. This clay is like the past, the walking people are the present, and everything above is the future: both history for the ages and that unearthly continuation for the souls of the people making the Exodus, whose life did not end with what was on earth. The Exodus is also you and me. We all walk in this life into the unknown, like the heroes of Oleksandr Antonyuk's picture. What matters is that each of us can come out of his own Egypt, that our way leads from darkness to light, and that our names are one day written in the Book of Life.

Author: Alla Varentsova. Translated from Russian.
