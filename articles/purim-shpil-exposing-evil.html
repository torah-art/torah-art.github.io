The Bible and the Artist

Purim-shpil. Exposing Evil…

2015 · The Bible and the Artist, Biblical Feasts

How acceptable is it for a believer to portray evil on stage?

The Jewish tradition of staging special theatrical performances at Purim, the "PURIM-SHPIL", is one of the important and much-loved parts of this feast.

It was the occasion for today's conversation. My reflections are on how acceptable it is for a believer to portray evil on stage. I mean such servants of evil as Hitler, Haman, demons, Satan himself and so on. The question also arises in the other arts, wherever the portrayal of evil is involved.

In the theatrical performances for Purim, besides the wonderful and blessed characters of Mordechai and his niece Esther, the Book of Esther also has Haman, the bitterest enemy of the Jewish people, a descendant of Amalek, evil incarnate.

The video recording of the conversation.

It is interesting to hear the expert opinion of professionals in the theatre, wonderful artists, actors of the Ecclesiastes Drama Theatre in Yaroslavl, Sergey Komyagin and Sergey Lukyanov. Sergey Komyagin is also a playwright and the theatre's director, and his colleague Sergey Lukyanov is also the youth pastor of the "Church of God" congregation.

Sergey Komyagin: It is all a matter of attitude. When we were taught at drama school, we were always told that an actor needs a personal opinion about the characters he plays. Put simply, when you play any character, good or bad, the first thing you need to know about yourself is: who are you? The prosecutor or the defence counsel of your role? This matters especially when your character is a villain, Hitler, say. After all, Hitler was a terrible man. In a certain sense he was Satan himself from the underworld. And someone has to play that kind of role too. But from the start, what is your attitude to him as an actor? What is your civic position towards this character?

And our civic position on this is unchanging. What can we, as holy people, think about devils, demons, or Hitler?

Of course my personal attitude is utterly negative! But my task is to play this character, and I turn him in such a way as to evoke a particular attitude in people. Either I want people to fall in love with him, or to curse him, to see what an odious figure he is, what a scoundrel. So my task is to turn this character so that people simply spit and say: what a lousy reptile! How does the earth bear such a man? And the more I show the extreme side of evil in him, the greater the revulsion I evoke. And by that I justify myself. That's what we actors say: to justify yourself in the profession. In this case I justify myself by what I did with the character: I simply exposed him. I evoked the utmost indignation and hatred towards him.

When we play devils and demons, we have our own inner position towards these characters.

If I were to justify Hitler, if I made a film about Hitler that justified him, that said what a good man he was, that he was really the "orderly of the forest" and in his own way was cleansing the earth of evil, then I myself would be a fascist. But if my overarching task from the start is to show that Nazism is evil and Hitler is evil, I can play Hitler very seriously and in doing so show how odious a figure he is. It is simply a question of genre, of what is better for me in a particular situation.

Sergey Lukyanov: Religion tells a person: "This is bad, that is bad. It is bad to portray this or that." But in fact what matters is who does it, and the purpose for which I do it. If I want to sow corruption in the land, if I want to pervert people, then it really is bad. If I want to lead people to the truth, what does it matter if I have antlers like a deer? I will lead them to the truth. That's the whole secret.

Sergey Komyagin: In the final part of the story of Esther all the enemies are overthrown. Haman was hanged, and his ten sons were punished too. And at the end of this story, as of any other, the viewer watching a film or a play, or reading a book, must in the end receive the deepest satisfaction from the fact that evil has been punished. And what does this satisfaction depend on? It can be achieved to a greater or lesser degree.

My task is for a person to receive the greatest satisfaction from evil being punished. And he will receive it in full only when I bring that evil vividly to light. After all, when a villain is shown on television or in the cinema and he does a great deal of evil, people will get no satisfaction from the film if he goes unpunished. He must be punished, and punished in proportion to his evil. Otherwise people will leave the cinema extremely dissatisfied, because he did evil one hundred per cent and was punished only twenty. So we must not allow him to be punished less.

So the more I play Haman as a villain, the more I show him as the lowest of the low, the greater the satisfaction people will have when deserved retribution overtakes him.

So my task as an actor is to satisfy people's sense of justice, their sense of righteous anger. So I really must give myself to it so completely that, if I play him, I play him as such a scoundrel and villain as the earth has never yet produced. And that way I fulfil the overarching task. Because in a work there is a concept called the overarching task. My task is to play Haman as a villain, because the overarching task is to make sure evil is punished. And the more I bring this evil to light, the greater the satisfaction and joy that at last the earth is rid of it.

In general, by the laws of drama, for good to win in the end, it must practically lose. The work must be built so that evil seems just about to win. And only at the last moment, when evil has almost won, must good prevail. That raises the price of victory. If good had pinned evil to the ground straight away, the price of victory would not be the same. Why do people cry at the end? Why is victory always with tears in one's eyes? Because a very great price was paid. That is the law of drama. For the defeat of evil to be impressive and to satisfy people, evil must practically win, the power of darkness everywhere. And then, in the last second, victory! One last push and good wins.

Sergey Lukyanov: The greatest actor and director is God!

Sergey Komyagin: Even in this story of Haman. Everything looks lost. The documents are ready, the seals are set, the gallows fifty cubits high is ready with its noose, and it seems it's all over… And suddenly, in the last second, when everyone thought the struggle was lost, at the very last moment: the greatest victory! And the victory becomes all the more precious because everything was on the brink of defeat. I understand people's wish to play the hero. But I also understand the wish of an actor who wants to play the dark force in order to help the hero and bring him out even more. That's why there have always been these characters: the red-haired clown and the white clown. There has to be a red-haired clown, because without him the white clown won't "fire". And the other way round. They need each other.

It is the same here: heroes need darkness, they need an anti-hero. Because without an anti-hero the hero won't "fire".

Sergey Lukyanov: I would love to play Haman. I really would. I would try to show how he tries to accuse and slander, because that is his trait: making people guilty. I would love to show his meanness. I just don't know whether I fit the part.

There are no bad roles. There is only a wrong approach to a role.

Ephraim Kalish: In interviews with various actors I have more than once heard that it is dangerous to play unclean spirits, that through it one can get into very serious trouble and have quite unexpected side effects.

Sergey Lukyanov: When I was young I played a boy who in the course of the play went out of his mind, became a little deranged. When the play ended, the audience was in tears. My last line was the half-mad boy saying: "No more blood, enough blood…" A flat, emotionless line. After that role I could not come to myself for two days.

And afterwards a wonderful man told me: "That is the first sign of unprofessionalism, that you got inside the role." He said that an actor must always be, as it were, watching from outside. Because if a person lives himself into a role, he is in danger. For example, in the play "Spiritual Warfare" I play Satan (see the play here, in Russian). But I am not playing Satan. I am a preacher who, in the image of Satan, exposes his kingdom! So can you imagine: we were being filled with the Holy Spirit! Can you imagine, I'm in costume as the devil, with horns, and we were being filled with the Holy Spirit. Because we were preaching. We were not the devil. We portrayed him, but we were not that ourselves. We were the prosecutors of this role. That's why people laughed at the kingdom of darkness, at the wiles of evil, because they had been exposed.

One more thing that helped me: it depends on the faith of the actors. You can live yourself into a role and go mad, a poltergeist can settle in your home, if a person does not understand the purpose of what he is doing. Artists and any creative person, even a mathematician who has nothing to do with acting, can go off their heads, even a physicist… anyone. You can be a plumber and find devils in a pipe: just add a bit more fanaticism :) and religiosity.
