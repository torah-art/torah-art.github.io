Artifacts

Isaac Itkind

2015 · Artifacts, The Bible and the Artist

Author: Eduard Topol. Translated from Russian.

In 1967, at the Bolshevo film-makers' retreat outside Moscow, a young documentary director from Kazakhstan, Ararat Mashanov, was showing the leading lights of the capital's cinema his 20-minute documentary "A Touch of Eternity", about Isaac Itkind, a sculptor famous in the thirties who had outlived his official death.

On the screen a stocky little old man, five feet tall, 96 years old, with a huge white beard, looking like Sabaoth or a Christmas gnome, was walking busily among enormous wooden and plaster sculptures, working on them with a chisel, and his eyes shone with lively, youthful mischief. Meanwhile the narrator was saying that in the thirties Isaac Itkind had been as famous as Chagall, Erzia and Konenkov, and that Itkind's sculptures stand in the museums of France, West Germany, the USA and... in the storerooms of the Russian Museum in Leningrad and the Pushkin Museum in Moscow.

At this point the camera moved into a museum storeroom, and here came the most striking detail of the film. We saw a two-metre wooden sculpture of Alexander Pushkin: a young, slender, graceful, inspired and, I would say, radiant Sasha Pushkin at the rising of his genius. The whole sculpture was impulse, freshness, life, poetry. And below, on the pedestal, the camera stopped for a second on a short inscription: "Sculptor Isaac Itkind. 1871–1938". And that was all. The narrator did not say a word. The camera gently moved away from the inscription and again showed us Itkind's life in Alma-Ata, but from then on the whole film was lit for us by the meaning of that short inscription: for all the museums of the world the life of the brilliant sculptor Isaac Itkind had broken off in Stalin's camps in 1938.

A few months later I found myself in Alma-Ata on a journalistic assignment. The "city of apples", beautiful as Vienna, lies not far from the Chinese border and is surrounded by the snowy peaks of the Pamirs. Half the population is Kazakh, the other half Russian, and there is a huge number of mixed marriages; from this mixing the streets are full of amazingly beautiful girls, with white skin and slightly slanting black eyes...

At the Union of Kazakh Artists they told me that Itkind was ill, had a cold, and lived at the back of beyond, on the outskirts of Alma-Ata, in a flat with no telephone. But after a glance at my Komsomolskaya Pravda press card, the Union's young secretary, Nadya, agreed to take me to him. And now a taxi is rolling into the snowbound Alma-Ata "Cheryomushki", a housing estate of six-storey prefabricated "khrushchobas", built in a hurry in the days of Khrushchev's struggle with the catastrophic housing crisis in the USSR. On the way Nadya tells me about Itkind.

In 1944 rumours began to go round Alma-Ata about some half-wild old man, a gnome or a sorcerer, who lived on the edge of the city, in the ground, ate roots, gathered tree stumps in the woods and made amazing figures out of them. Children, who in those war years roamed the waste ground and the suburbs unsupervised, said that these wooden figures really wept and really laughed...

After a while the rumours became so persistent that the heads of the Kazakh Art Fund decided to have a look at these "living figures made of stumps". Several well-known Kazakh artists, among them the painter Nikolai Mukhin, went to the outskirts of Alma-Ata, to Golovnoi Aryk. Today this street has become Abai Avenue, but then cattle grazed there.

The artists wandered a long time over the waste ground and at last saw what they were looking for. In a clay hill something like a dugout had been made; a hole, narrow as a mole's, led into the depth of the burrow. Near the entrance lay stumps and pieces of wood only just touched by a woodcarver's chisel. But the artists, being professionals, understood from these first outlines that something remarkable was about to open before them.

They went up to the hole leading into the dugout. From inside came the light tapping of a hammer on a chisel. One of the artists bent down and shouted into the burrow: "Hey!" A small, grey-haired old man of 73 crawled out of the dugout. He was hard of hearing and spoke terribly ungrammatical Russian, with a monstrous Jewish accent. But when he told the artists his name, they started.

Before them stood Isaac Itkind, a sculptor who only eight or ten years earlier had been as famous in the USSR as Marc Chagall and Picasso are famous in France today. Almost every newspaper had written about him then; famous writers and directors were his friends: Maxim Gorky, Vladimir Mayakovsky, Sergei Yesenin, Vsevolod Meyerhold, Vasily Kachalov; the pillars of Soviet power looked after him: the People's Commissar of Education Anatoly Lunacharsky and the first secretary of the Leningrad regional party committee Sergei Kirov. And exhibitions of his sculpture were events in the cultural life of pre-war Russia.

Now this Itkind, whose name had been a textbook name for them since their student years, lived in some mole's burrow, starved, lived on roots and alms and... made sculptures.

"Why? How did you end up here?" the artists asked.
"They arrested me in '37 and exiled me first to Siberia, then here, to Kazakhstan. Now they have let me out of the camp, because I am already too old for them. But they let me out without the right to return to Moscow. They said I had been given exile for life..."
"What were you put away for?"
"For being an enemy of the people, a Japanese spy. I sold Japan the secrets of the Baltic Fleet," Itkind answered, and asked with an indescribable Jewish intonation: "Can you believe it?"

Of course they could not believe that this world-famous sculptor, this little gnome of genius with a monstrous Jewish accent, was a Japanese spy, or that he understood anything at all about the military secrets of the Baltic Fleet. But in 1944 in the USSR people declared "enemies of the people" by Stalin's regime were treated like lepers. So nothing changed in the life of the exiled "enemy of the people" and "Japanese spy" Isaac Itkind. Except that one of the artists who visited him then, Nikolai Mukhin, did dare to climb into his burrow and pulled a large wooden sculpture out of the dugout.

It was a sketch of "The Laughing Old Man", a sculpture that twenty years later would become one of Itkind's most famous works.

"We'll take it to our Fund's museum, may we?" the artists said to Itkind. Itkind allowed it; they loaded the sculpture into the car and drove off, feeling almost like heroes: after all, they had taken a sculpture from an "enemy of the people" for the museum!

"Itkind stood at the entrance to the dugout and waved after us," Nikolai Mukhin later recalled. He lived in that dugout for another twelve (do you hear: twelve!) years. Only now and then, and in secret, did Nikolai Mukhin visit him and give him a little money...

Then came Stalin's death, the Twentieth Party Congress, the rehabilitation of millions of "enemies of the people". By that time Itkind had been completely forgotten again. And who would look after an exiled old man for years when such things were going on all around: the post-war devastation, then a new wave of arrests in 1948. Merely for associating with an exiled "enemy of the people" one could get ten years in the camps...

How he lived those years, on what, and whether he lived at all, no one knew or cared... So when in the winter of 1956 a homeless little old man of 85 came to the Alma-Ata State Theatre, no one recognised him as the famous sculptor Itkind. In that year, 1956, there were hundreds of thousands of such ragged old men just released from the Siberian and Kazakh camps. Some of them were straining to get from Siberia to their home cities, to the European part of Russia, to Moscow, Leningrad, Kiev: to their children, wives and relatives. But tens of thousands more were in no hurry to go anywhere: none of their family was left alive; their wives and children had forgotten, abandoned or betrayed them in their time... These wandered around the places of their former imprisonment or exile and looked for work there. And all of them, by their own account, had been famous before their arrest.

That year, 1956, Alma-Ata was flooded with them. From the Karaganda mines, from the copper mines of Dzhezkazgan, from the camps of Aktyubinsk...

The small, homeless, gnome-like old man begged the director of the Alma-Ata theatre to take him on to paint scenery and daub backdrops. He said that now, when the title of "enemy of the people" and the ban on living in big cities had been lifted from him, he would still not go to Moscow or Leningrad: there was no one to go to. And here, in Kazakhstan, he had got used to things and settled in. And the director took him on as a house painter at 60 roubles a month and even gave him "lodging": a plank bed under the theatre staircase, where the theatre's doorkeeper, Sonya Yefimovna, usually warmed herself by the stove...

For two years the old man climbed the theatre stepladders, daubing backdrops and scenery for the plays from a local artist's sketches. And in his free time he wandered round the outskirts of Alma-Ata and dragged huge stumps and snags back to the theatre cellar on passing lorries and tip-trucks. Soon all the lorry drivers in Alma-Ata knew that at the city theatre there was some old eccentric who would give three roubles, the price of a bottle of vodka, for a wooden snag or stump. Naturally, stumps and snags began to arrive at the theatre from almost all over Kazakhstan.

And at night Isaac Itkind, armed with a chisel, a hammer and a gouge, went down into the cellar and set to work. No one disturbed him; no one except the theatre doorkeeper, Sonya Yefimovna, heard the tapping of his hammer on the chisel. And only two years later a new young artist at the theatre looked into the cellar and gasped: there stood twenty unique wooden sculptures, certainly made by a major, if not a great, master.

The artist asked the old man his name and remembered that he had heard it at the art institute in lectures on the history of Soviet fine art. Of course! There had been a trio of wood sculptors famous in the thirties: Konenkov, Erzia and Itkind. Konenkov was alive and had become an academician; Erzia had died; and Itkind...

So in Kazakhstan Isaac Itkind was found "again".

The young artists of Alma-Ata flocked to the theatre cellar to gape at the celebrity risen from the dead. The young and energetic Kazakh poet Olzhas Suleimenov and several other well-known writers and artists began to petition for the old man to be admitted to the Union of Artists, and then... then fame came to Itkind. Fame on a local, Kazakh scale, it is true.

That was the time of the opening up of the Virgin Lands. Khrushchev had announced that in twenty years the USSR would catch up with and overtake America in the production of grain, milk and meat per head of population. In this race he gave a special role to the development of the wild virgin lands of Kazakhstan, where several million young workers and billions of roubles were thrown. By Khrushchev's plan the virgin lands of Kazakhstan were to feed Russia with bread. And so new towns and settlements sprang up here like mushrooms: Tselinograd, Pavlodar, Semipalatinsk.

Khrushchev did not stint on money for these towns; they even acquired their own museums and art galleries. The collections and exhibitions for these museums were assembled by young art historians, graduates of the Moscow and Leningrad art institutes. It was they who, on learning of the resurrected Itkind, bought his sculptures for their museums. Then Itkind was admitted to the Union of Artists of Kazakhstan, received the prize of the Central Committee of the Lenin Komsomol of the Kazakh Republic and (even!) a two-room flat on the outskirts of Alma-Ata.

Of course, this struggle of the young Kazakh intelligentsia for Itkind had its subtext. As if to say: the Russians in Moscow ruined a great sculptor, and we Kazakhs are saving him for history! And they really did save him; they literally pulled him out from under the back staircase, gave him a prize and moved him into a decent flat. More than that, they got the city military commissariat to allow Itkind to set up a studio in the cellar bomb shelter of the building where he was given the flat. And they made a film about him...

Here my guide Nadya broke off her story and said we ought to buy a bottle of sweet wine: Itkind may be 96, but he would gladly drink a glass of sweet wine. And in general, Nadya said, the old man liked it when people came to him with wine and young girls.

"Two months ago," she went on with a smile, "Itkind was taken to hospital with pneumonia. I came to visit him and began to help the nurse wheel him on his bed from the ward to the X-ray room. He had a temperature of 39.2°, but, imagine it, when he opened his eyes in the hospital corridor on the way and saw that two young girls were wheeling his bed, something stirred under the sheet: down there, you know, below the stomach..."

Of course, I stopped the taxi at a shop and bought a bottle of wine, and then we drove for another twenty minutes or so through the snowbound streets of Alma-Ata...

And here we are at Itkind's. In the cold two-room flat, on a bed by the window, lay not a white-bearded Sabaoth at all, but a beardless little old man with thin grey hair, very like either an imp or a house spirit from Vrubel's painting "Pan". This was Itkind. His grumbling and slovenly dressed wife, that same former theatre doorkeeper Sonya Yefimovna, glancing sideways unkindly at young Nadya, put on tea...

But Itkind, seeing Nadya, seemed to rise up above the bed. His eyes at once lit up and grew younger; the wrinkles on his face, round as a baked apple, came alive. He took Nadya by the hand, sat her beside him on the bed and said to her, with the same strong, unconquered Jewish accent:

"Yesterday they brought me a wonderful piece of wood! Oh, what wood! Oh! Come, I'll show you, it's outside under the snow. If you will sit for me, I'll make a sculpture of 'Spring' out of it! Come! Come!"

And in spite of our protests he got up, put on a quilted jacket and trousers, pushed his feet into felt boots and led us out into the yard. There he walked round and round in the snow, literally with longing, a thick five-metre snag of wood, repeating:

"You see? No, just look what a wonderful wood! Oh, what wood! Nadya, will you sit for me? It will be 'Spring', a real one! Oh, what a 'Spring' I'll make! Oh, what a one!"

Then he led us into the cellar bomb shelter, and here I saw a metre-high plaster head of Maxim Gorky; a thirty-year-old Alexander Pushkin in wood; a dozen wooden girls' torsos of various sizes all called "Spring", airy, as if flying; and... an almost metre-high plaster head of Lenin.

Here I could not help asking:
"You are sculpting Lenin?! YOU?! After almost thirty years inside?"
"Yes," he said. "But it doesn't come out the way I want yet..."

And I began to come to Isaac Itkind every evening, never forgetting to bring a bottle of sweet wine and also Nadya or some other young girl. Sipping the wine and casting keen, young glances at the pretty guest, Itkind willingly told me his life in detail, and I wrote it down, understanding that I was writing down a unique novel worthy of the pen of Lion Feuchtwanger or Stefan Zweig.

...Isaac Itkind was born on 9 April 1871 in the Hasidic shtetl of Smorgon in the province of Vilna. His father Yaakov was a Hasidic rabbi, and Isaac of course followed in his father's footsteps: how could it be otherwise in a family of hereditary Hasidic rabbis? He finished yeshiva and became a rabbi like his father, but when he was 26, a book about the sculptor M. Antokolsky, famous at the time, came into his hands by chance. The book had illustrations, among them photographs of Antokolsky's well-known high reliefs "The Tailor" and "An Old Man's Evening Work". Itkind at once recognised in these old men people he knew from the shtetl: there was exactly such a tailor in Smorgon, and in exactly the same way another old man, leaning out of a window, screwed up his eyes in the evening light to thread a needle in the rays of the setting sun...

This book, which he read syllable by syllable because it was printed in Russian, gave Isaac no peace. It turned out that the famous Antokolsky, who amazed viewers with such powerful sculptures as "Ivan the Terrible", "Spinoza" and "Christ Before the People", that very Antokolsky, was also a Jew, and more than that, a fellow countryman of Itkind's, from Vilna. The young rabbi could find no rest...

Meanwhile in the shtetl another small shtetl drama was coming to an end: the local rich man Pinya, owner of the hardware shop, had at last married off his only daughter, the hunchback Bronya.

"Oh, it was a very long story," Itkind told me. "No one wanted to marry Bronya, she was so ugly. She was shorter than me, and a hunchback, can you imagine. Pinya was offering a very big dowry for her, but even the shop assistants in Pinya's shop, who would sell their souls to the devil for a penny, turned Bronya down. But we had a porter in Smorgon, Khatsel. A bogatyr, as the Russians say. He could lift two sacks of flour. He would heave a log of ten poods onto his shoulder and carry it alone wherever it was needed. But he was a shlimazl. Do you know what a shlimazl is? The children would shout to him in the street: 'Khantsel! I'll give you two kopecks! Be a horse!' And Khantsel, who earned twice as much as the other porters, would go down on all fours, the children would climb onto his back, and he would carry them round the shtetl like a horse. Not for the money. But because he could not refuse anyone. He was more than kind; he was a shlimazl. And so when all the shtetl suitors had turned Bronya down, her father Pinya came to Khantsel. And Khantsel did not refuse Pinya. And there was a wedding. And the young couple walked through the shtetl: huge Khantsel, two metres tall, and the little hunchback Bronya. I saw them walking down the street. I did not know whether to laugh or cry. I sat and thought of nothing. I just kneaded clay in my hands, and came to myself only when the figures of the young couple, Khantsel and Bronya, stood on the table before me.

"After that I completely lost my head. I left the synagogue and went to Vilna. I wanted to study to be a sculptor, but found only work as a bookbinder's apprentice. Two years later I returned to our shtetl, but our Hasidim already considered me almost a goy: after all, I had abandoned religion, I had lost God. More than that, I modelled people out of clay, and that is forbidden by the Jewish religion: no one has the right to do what God did... You're not tired?"

"No, we're not tired, we're listening..."
"All the same, let's have another little glass... My beauty, will you sit for me?.."

The Hasidim of Smorgon considered him a renegade, an outcast. The old men spat as they passed his house. But one day their local writer, Peretz Hirschbein, came into the house. He silently examined Itkind's sculptures and left. And a few days later an article by Hirschbein appeared in a newspaper. He wrote that in Smorgon there lived a natural talent who was creating masterpieces.

And those same Hasidim who had spat on the gate of the Itkinds' house sent a delegate round the shtetl. The delegate went from house to house, showed the illiterate craftsmen the newspaper with the article about Itkind and collected money so that "this shlimazl Isaac" could go and study to be "a real sculptor". And he went: first to Vilna, to the Vilna art school, and then to Moscow.

"Jews were forbidden to live in the big cities then, especially in Moscow," Itkind told me. "Only young Jewish women, if they registered with the gendarmerie as prostitutes and got the 'yellow ticket', could live in Moscow. And so there were many young Jewish girls then who formally registered as prostitutes while actually going to study at an institute or taking a job.

"But every six months they had to re-register with the gendarmerie. And then they would move out of one flat, find a room in another part of Moscow and go to another police station, as if they had just arrived and wanted to become prostitutes. It was with these girls that I lived, now with one, now with another, and I went to take the entrance examination at the Moscow School of Painting, Sculpture and Architecture."

The well-known professor and monumental sculptor Sergei Volnukhin, whose works still adorn Moscow and Petersburg (for example, the monument to the Russian first printer Ivan Fyodorov in the very centre of Moscow, by the Kremlin), gave Itkind an examination task: to sculpt a figure of a woman. Itkind had never seen a nude model before: where would one come from in Smorgon? But the young rabbi overcame this "obstacle" too. For two months he lived wherever he could, hiding from the police, and after two months he presented his work to the professor.

"Volnukhin said nothing to me. He called a phaeton, loaded my sculpture into the phaeton and took it to Maxim Gorky. Gorky was already a famous writer then. And Gorky liked my work so much that he got into the same phaeton, and the two of them, he and the professor, went to the governor of Moscow. They asked this governor to allow me to live and study in Moscow. 'A Jew, a talented artist?! It cannot be!' this governor told them. 'Jews can be talented in commerce, that I understand. But not in art!' And he refused Gorky himself, can you imagine? But I stayed in Moscow, illegally. By day I worked as a locksmith, at night I sculpted, and I lived here and there, and soon I became famous, really! Because Gorky went everywhere and said: 'Itkind, Itkind, Itkind...' And he made me famous. People began to buy my works; even Savva Morozov bought my works! I had exhibitions; I was admitted to the Union of Artists. And then came the revolution. Oh, how glad I was! Now I could live freely in Moscow without police permission: there were no police any more! True, soon the famine began. So what? I still worked a great deal. I made my best things then: 'My Father', 'The Rabbi', 'Longing', 'The Talmudist', 'The Tzaddik', 'Jewish Melody', 'The Kabbalist'... Forty-two of my sculptures were in my one-man exhibition at the Jewish theatre Habima in 1918.

"Theodore Roosevelt's brother came to Russia then; he was at my exhibition, and afterwards he came to my studio and bought all the works there were in it. He invited me to America; it was very easy to leave for America then. He said that in America I would be very famous and would earn millions. And do you know what I answered him? I told him that other artists could leave for America, because even under the tsar they had been people in Russia. But under the tsar I was a person only until six in the evening, and after six in the evening any policeman could arrest me. And now, when the revolution has made me a person after six in the evening too, how can I leave? That is what I answered him... And the famine? What is famine! When the real famine began, Maxim Gorky obtained for me from the People's Commissar Lunacharsky a professor's ration: coupons for dried roach and bread. True, when I came to Tsekubu, I had to fill in some form there, and I could not write Russian. Of course the commissar could not believe that a professor could not write Russian. They decided I was a swindler and put me under arrest. But then Lunacharsky telephoned them, and they let me go and gave me the ration..."

The next twenty years were perhaps the happiest in Itkind's life. That does not mean they were cloudless. In the hungry post-revolutionary years he went hungry, because he traded his professor's ration for plaster and wood.

In 1926 he began to cough blood, and on the advice of Mikhoels, the great Jewish actor who was later murdered by KGB agents on Stalin's orders, Itkind went to the south of Russia, to the Black Sea, to Simferopol. Here too he wandered without a roof and lived on odd jobs and small fees. But he worked a great deal; for days on end he did not leave his studio in the attic of a house in which, by a whim of fate, the Simferopol branch of the GPU (as the KGB was called in those years) was located below. In this attic he made a unique sculptural group, "The Pogrom", which stunned viewers with its power.

In 1930 a huge article about Itkind appeared in Krasnaya Gazeta. The journalist wrote: "At the sculptor's I saw a photograph of 'The Pogrom', a huge sculptural group that perished in a fire in his studio. It is truly a work of stunning expressiveness. Set up in a square, it could, by the force of its artistic effect, do more in the struggle against antisemitism than ten thousand logical and moral arguments against it..."

And this was not the only article about Itkind; there were many, and all were crowned with dramatic headlines like these: "A Starving Sculptor", "Why Is the Sculptor Itkind Starving?", "An Artist Who Must Be Supported", "I Call on the Soviet Public"...

But even with such headlines, shouting from the pages of Soviet newspapers that a great sculptor was starving, this was fame, recognition.

In 1937 Russia marked the centenary of the death of Alexander Pushkin, killed in a duel. The Hermitage announced a competition for the best sculpture of Pushkin. Hundreds of works were shown at the exhibition.

First prize went to three sculptures by Itkind: "The Young Pushkin", of which I spoke at the beginning of this chapter; "Alexander Pushkin", the poet in the last years of his life; and "Pushkin Dying", a simple and phenomenal work: the head of the dying poet on a pillow. This work cannot be put into words! You see the face of a man already calmed by death: the eyes are closed, the wrinkles on the forehead have straightened deathly smooth, and only the corners of the lips are still tormented by terrible pain... Pain and bitterness...

"When I work," Itkind remarked, "I think: this will be my very best. And when I finish, I no longer like it. I think: I should have done it not like this but like that. But the dying Pushkin was very good! Because I understood him; I understood how he died, how he suffered. I modelled his face and wept myself. I thought I had fallen ill myself. My wife was frightened and sent for the doctor... Listen, I have lived so long, so long... Every morning I wake up, open my eyes and wonder: can I really still be alive? I think I am sure to go to paradise: after all, there will be plenty of nude models and wood from the trees of paradise there. I always lacked them so much on earth. And there will be as much free time as you like. But do you know, I am still afraid to die. I have lived almost a hundred years, and still I am afraid. Do you know why? What if they bury an old woman of eighty next to me, and I have to lie with her for all eternity?! Eh? Now my wife is old, only thirty years younger than me; you've seen her, it's Sonya, who worked as the doorkeeper at the theatre. She doesn't need anything except money, money! Because she's old! But then, before my arrest, before '37, I had a young wife, she was 26, oh, what a beauty, oh! A journalist! I was 66 and she was 26, can you imagine?! Forty years' difference, not even thirty, but that was something quite different. How she loved me, oh, how she loved me! She followed me to Siberia, to the camp. She passed me bread through the barbed wire... And then she died of typhus. Here, in Kazakhstan..."

And Itkind, who had just been laughing at death, hunger, paradise and hell, quietly sat down on a stump, an old gnarled snag of wood, and seemed to merge with it, to become himself a sculpture of eternity. Only his hands, small, dark, strong hands, wandered almost mechanically over the knots and joints of this snag, alive for him, feeling for something in it: something that tomorrow would come alive under his chisel for everyone...

I was looking at his hands, and he caught my glance.

"It's a pity I'm ill and cannot work. I cannot not work. A director died here recently. He used to come to me often, and I saw that he would die soon. Because they no longer let him work. A director is a very bad profession. You cannot work alone. But I have a very good profession; no one can take my work away from me.

"Do you know why I survived in prison? They arrested me and put me in the Peter and Paul Fortress, in a cellar, in solitary, and for eight months a KGB investigator beat me every day; he even burst the eardrum in my left ear. He kept demanding that I write that I was a Japanese spy and what secrets of the Baltic Fleet I had sold to Japan. And I could not write it, because I could not write Russian. And then they beat me again, and again... Do you know how I survived? I survived because I have a very good profession. They gave me one little piece of black bread a day. In the morning they gave me a piece of bread for the whole day. But I did not eat that bread till night. All day I modelled little figures out of that bread. Only in the evening before sleep did I eat the bread. The next day they beat me again, but they still gave me bread, and so I could model all day and not think about them. You understand, I did not think about them! They tried to frighten me, but I did not think about fear; I modelled. And those who thought about them all day wrote against themselves, confessed that they were spies or had plotted to kill Stalin. And then they were shot at once. But I wrote nothing, and they sent me to Siberia, to a camp. There it was quite good for me: I worked at tree-felling, and there was a lot of wood around, and at night I could carve wood and make all sorts of sculptures and again not think about fear. Of course, when my wife died, everyone forgot about me, even my son. And it was not so good any more. Especially when they sent me here, to Kazakhstan. Here you had to pay for wood..."

I listened to Itkind and tried to imagine the road this small, great old man had travelled from the nineteenth century into the twentieth. From a Jewish ghetto in the province of Vilna, from being a rabbi in a shtetl synagogue, to an illegal life among Moscow prostitutes. And then, from the heights of fame, into the stone cellars of the Peter and Paul Fortress, the most famous Russian prison, where tsarist criminals had been held as early as the eighteenth century. Eight months of damp stone solitary, beatings every day and a piece of black bread for the whole day. And then the Siberian and Kazakh camps, total oblivion for 30 years. Thirty years is a whole life!..

In that same year, 1967, when I met Itkind, the book "Papillon" came out in the West and became an international bestseller: the memoirs of the French convict Henri Charrière. The book described Papillon's adventures and torments in the penal prisons of French Guiana and his heroic attempts to escape. Like Itkind, Papillon also once spent several months in a dark stone solitary cell, receiving only a piece of black bread for the whole day. And, like Itkind, he did not eat that bread in the morning but divided it into parts so as to last the whole day on it. Of course, he was not a sculptor and did not model figures out of that bread; he had another passion that helped him survive: the dream of escape and of revenge on the French prosecutor who had sent him to penal servitude.

This book was translated into almost every language in the world; millions of copies were sold in hardback and paperback; critics and readers compared Papillon with the Count of Monte Cristo, and Hollywood made a film of the book with Anthony Quinn in the leading role.

Isaac Itkind went through the same trials as Papillon, not in the tropics of French Guiana but in Stalin's prisons and in the Siberian and Kazakh camps. He came to this penal servitude not as a healthy young man, like Papillon, but at 66. What helped him survive and defeat the KGB was not dreams of escape and revenge but his calling. In practice, all those 30 years he was on the run from them: from the investigators, from the executioners, from fear, from Stalin's terror. He fled from them to a piece of bread he could model, to the snags and stumps out of which he made sculptures, living even underground, like a mole...

He was free: free of socialism, totalitarianism, Stalinism. And now he told me about it as simply and matter-of-factly as perhaps only Anthony Quinn could have played it, if he had known of the existence of Isaac Itkind...

"And now in Alma-Ata life has become quite good for me again," Itkind told me. "I have a great deal of wood now, you see?"

We were sitting with him in his "studio", the cold bomb shelter; Itkind was in felt boots and a quilted jacket, but I saw that he really was content with what he had: so lovingly did he look at the wooden blocks, stumps and snags that lay around us on the floor of his studio and out there in the street under the snow, lying and waiting for his hands and his chisel.

"Yes, it is very good for me here. I have a lot of wood and a lot of work thought out. Young people come to me and watch me work. And Jews come to me from the synagogue, and I dictate my book to them in Yiddish. A book about the meaning of life. After all, everything in life has meaning. For example, when a woman has a child, she has milk, doesn't she? Not earlier and not later. Everything in nature has its meaning, and everything is rightly devised. And I need to work. A man needs to work, to do his work: in a camp, in prison, it makes no difference. Then he lives: that too is devised by nature. Or by God... Yes, I think a lot now about nature and about God, the Jewish God, from whom I ran away seventy years ago..."

Isaac Itkind, the famous sculptor whose works stand in the best museums of the world, and also in the museums of Semipalatinsk, Pavlodar, Tselinograd and Alma-Ata, and lie in the cellars of the Russian Museum and the Hermitage in Leningrad and the Pushkin Museum in Moscow, became after his ninetieth birthday a member of the Union of Soviet Artists and received the title "Honoured Art Worker of the Kazakh Soviet Socialist Republic". He died in Alma-Ata on 14 February 1969, at the age of 98. I hope the old Jews of the Alma-Ata synagogue have kept the book on the meaning of life that Itkind dictated before his death.

P.S. Recently, in March 2011, in Moscow I met Arkady Naumovich Itkind, a professor of economics and Isaac Itkind's grandson. We sat in the Pyramid café on Tverskaya, drank green tea and talked about his celebrated grandfather and about how a film ought to be made: a full-length documentary about the great twentieth-century sculptor Isaac Itkind, a film that should be seen not only in Russia but in all civilised countries. And his works should also be brought out of the storerooms, gathered into one travelling exhibition and taken round the world. And then, I am sure, Isaac Itkind will have a third life, an eternal one...

Moscow, 2011
