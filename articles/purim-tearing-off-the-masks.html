Biblical Feasts

Purim. Tearing Off the Masks

2015 · Biblical Feasts

It is interesting that this whole feast, and the Book of Esther as a whole, could be called a book of disguises. We have already seen these masks in the names of the main characters, Mordechai and Esther, whose Hebrew name is Hadassah. And further on we see a whole series of disguisings, dressings-up and tearings-off of masks.

Megillat Esther (the Book of Esther) tells of events of some two and a half thousand years ago (the Persian period, 539–331 BC). The Jews who remained in exile after the decree of Cyrus, king of Persia, to rebuild the house of God in Yerushalayim were living through hard times. Things went so far that the systematic destruction of all the Jews of the Persian kingdom was being prepared, with the full support of the authorities and even by their decree.

Video in Russian

A great deliverance came to the Jews of Persia from an unexpected quarter. In the role of a messiah, a saviour, a woman appears before us: Esther, a Jewess. Her second, Jewish name is Hadassah.

She and her uncle Mordechai issue a command to the Jews of the whole world to celebrate the miraculous deliverance from certain death, marking it with great joy and gladness. In time a tradition of merry masks and dressing-up arose.

Tearing off the masks

Ephraim Kalish: An interesting point runs through the book like a red thread: disguises, masks, changes of costume. The main characters of the book, Esther and Mordechai, are also masks of a kind. The name Mordechai (from the name of the Babylonian god Marduk) did not express who Mordechai was. We see confirmation of this in his behaviour when the order was issued that everyone must bow down before Haman. Mordechai's refusal was a matter of principle. Because he was a Jew "inside", "under the mask", a man faithful to the God of Avraham. Yes, he wore a not very beautiful mask on his face (a pagan name); Jews were often forced to put on such masks. But his inner core remained, and it did not allow him to bow to anyone except God Most High.

It is interesting that this whole feast, and the Book of Esther as a whole, could be called a book of disguises. We have already seen these masks in the names of the main characters, Mordechai and Esther, whose Hebrew name is Hadassah.

And further on we see a whole series of disguises.

Vadim Birman: There Esther changes clothes. She goes through what today we would call a year-long session at a beauty salon.

Ephraim Kalish: Yes, and her time in that salon ends with her being dressed in royal garments. And she receives royal dignity. In general, in Scripture clothing often expresses a person's position and dignity. Take, for example, the high priest or a Levite. All their garments were garments for standing before God. They pointed to their dignity, their standing before God and their place among Israel.

Vadim Birman: Clothing is always a sign, from ancient times to this day.

Ephraim Kalish: By the way, we have missed one more mask. Esther puts on another mask when her uncle Mordechai forbids her to reveal which people she comes from.

Vadim Birman: Right, that is another mask. The king does not know which people she belongs to.

Ephraim Kalish: In fact she puts on a mask, as it were, hiding her true face.

This is not an entirely holy act. We remember other such things in the Bible. Remember David?

Vadim Birman: When he pretended to be mad…

Ephraim Kalish: Yes, David letting his spittle run down his beard: that means throwing away one's dignity altogether. Though he could have performed "miracles of heroism" and… laid down his head.

In all this I see that, for all that, they were ordinary people and were sometimes afraid. But God turns even their fears to good. That is, in our case, the fact that she put on a mask, hiding her face, her belonging to the Jewish people, later became a weapon that Esther used.

Vadim Birman: God turned it into an advantage.

Ephraim Kalish: So after this disguise (hiding her face, her nationality), we see another one. Mordechai learns of Haman's intention, of how he has obtained from Achashverosh, the Persian king, a decree to destroy all the Jews and plunder their property. This was very serious! One way or another, antisemitism and hostility to the Jews have always (or almost always) gone together with some economic gain. This decree, which permitted the plunder of the property of the Jews who were to be destroyed, gave the population of Persia a powerful interest in the matter. Motivation is a great force.

When Mordechai learns of this terrible plot, he understands that a people scattered throughout the world, with no army of its own and unable to defend itself, can expect salvation from nowhere except from the Most High. And then he begins to cry out to God.
