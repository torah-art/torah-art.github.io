The Bible and the Artist

Biblical Symbolism in the Visual Arts

2015 · The Bible and the Artist

Author: Lilia Ratner (an excerpt from an article). Translated from Russian and published with the author's permission.

The use of symbols began in earnest with the spread of Christianity in the pagan world. It arose from the need to recognise those of like mind and also to hide the mysteries of the rites of worship from the pagans. Gradually Christian art worked out a whole system of symbols, and this circle included not only subjects from antiquity but also various images from the Old and New Testaments.

In time the range of symbolic objects connected with Christian art widened considerably. In paintings of the Passion of Christ literally every object carries a symbolic meaning: the thorn branches from which the crown was woven, the broken spear that pierced His heart, the cross, the pincers, the cup (chalice) and so on. In a painting of the Holy Family by the Netherlandish artist Joos van Cleve, the event is unfolded through objects that form a whole still life: the Child Jesus stands on a vine, which here signifies His coming death; a cut orange and a knife symbolise the tragedy to come. Joseph holds an open book, Mary a red carnation, a symbol of Christ's sufferings. In icons and paintings the Virgin Mary is sometimes shown with a distaff or spindle in her hands, a symbol of the new life beginning in her.

The Middle Ages forged the great language of the icon, fully adequate to the spirit of the Christian faith, a language in which everything is symbolic; but our subject is a different one. So-called "secular" art is also deeply symbolic, since all the genres of art came out of the icon: landscape is a memory of two gardens, Eden and Gethsemane. The portrait too was born of the idea of the "Countenance", turning first into a "face" and then into a "guise", a mask. The Dutch were especially fond of symbols.

In the 17th century, in the age of the Reformation, the genre of still life was born, in which every object is symbolic. This genre appeared after Calvin had driven all images out of the churches. But the need to express faith through images cannot be driven out of human culture. And the Dutch still life became a depiction of the Eucharist. The early still lifes are simple: a plank table, bread (the Body of Jesus Christ), wine (the Blood of Jesus Christ), a knife as the symbol of sacrifice. Later the still lifes filled with many objects, also symbolic. Thus the pomegranate is a symbol of the Church gathering souls into one. A half-peeled lemon is an unquenched thirst for life, and also a false friend hiding the bitterness of betrayal under the peel. A nut is a soul encased in the shell of sin. The luxurious vessels and dishes of later still lifes speak of human vanity and apostasy. This is already Belshazzar's feast, leading to death, not the Eucharist that gives life. And always, in almost every still life, a clock is hidden: a reminder that life is fleeting and existence perishable.

Symbolic objects in the still lifes of different periods are not unambiguous. Thus the fish is a symbol of the Divine meal and, in early Christianity, the password of Christians living among pagans (Ἰχθύς, Greek for fish, an ancient acronym of the first letters of the words Ἰησοῦς Χριστὸς Θεοῦ Υἱὸς Σωτήρ, Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour). In antiquity, by contrast, the mirror carp, like oysters, symbolised eroticism (Aphrodite was born from a shell).

In the landscapes and genre scenes of 17th-century Holland and Flanders we can also find many symbols. Thus the images of windmills go back to the idea of Fortune, eternal and cyclical movement. Sometimes windmills symbolise temperance, one of the seven Christian virtues, as in Pieter Bruegel the Elder. His beloved peasant scenes tell the viewer that there is an eternal cycle in nature, and that a successful grinding of grain depends on the wind just as human life depends on God's Providence. And in Pieter Bruegel the Younger's famous "Winter" the skaters symbolise the gliding of human life over ice. The hole in the ice and the bird trap are reminders of death, always near. Crows on the snow are harbingers of death. In David Teniers the Younger the jester is the embodiment of vanity, of the folly of the world, and at the same time of the possibility of "telling the truth to kings". His "Smokers", so peaceful, are a depiction of sin and vice.

In Jan de Heem the monkey is a symbol of the sin of lust, and sometimes, surrounded by various fruits, a depiction of the seasons. In Van Goyen's paintings a withered oak is the end of all things earthly, a kind of memento mori. The lizard and the snail stand for the mortality of all living things. The tulip is beauty that quickly fades.

Pieter Claesz also used symbols in his paintings. In his work the sea is life, the ship the Church, the sphere the fragility of existence, the finiteness of the world.

Images of a ladder are symbolic too. This symbol is of biblical origin: the ladder to heaven (Jacob's dream, Gen. 28:12). So in an icon the wooden ladder set against the cross is a meaningful image: the ladder leading to the cross is the way that gives salvation. In icons of the Mother of God, the ladder in her hands is a symbol of God's "descent" through her into the world, into His bodily incarnation. In the Renaissance the ladder turns into a pedestal, a kind of "podium of honour" raising the hero above the crowd. The pedestal is static; it leads nowhere; it is made for theatrical spectacle. In building a pedestal the point of view matters: from above or from below. The viewer's attitude to the hero depends on how the artist sees him.

The Tower of Babel is man's first presumptuous attempt to build his own ladder and climb to heaven. Fountains play an interesting part. Their appearance in painting is also symbolic. If in the icon the fountain is the source of life (the Mother of God as the "Life-giving Spring"), in Western European painting it is otherwise. Jets shooting upward, as if stopped by someone's hand, fall back down: from the Baroque (17th century) to the middle of the 19th century this was a playful model of the Tower of Babel. In the Northern Renaissance the silhouette of a half-ruined mountain is likewise a memory of the destruction of the Tower of Babel.

Yet often this image was understood as a desire to speak with God "face to face". One can draw an analogy with the Stylites (Mandelstam called the angel on the Alexander Column "the marble Stylite"). The triumphal column is also a development of the tower theme, but now as a sign of memory of earthly virtues.

A variant is the tower in scenes of the Annunciation as a fortress of virtue. Fortress towers in the distance are a symbol of mastery over space. It is a favourite motif of the Renaissance.

In later periods the tower ceases to be a symbol of standing in prayer and becomes a symbol of earth's opposition to heaven. The 19th and 20th centuries brought a completely new attitude to the "tower". The Eiffel Tower is a modern replica of the "Babel tower mania" in 19th-century architecture.

In the 20th century the unbuilt Palace of the Soviets is also a kind of collapsed virtual tower, where Lenin was to stand above the clouds, that is, to take the place of God.

Tatlin, the author of the "Tower of the Third International", tried to recreate this storming of heaven so characteristic of the 20th century. It illustrates well Mayakovsky's words: "Hey, you, Heaven, take off your hat, I'm coming!.." and "The angry hammer will pierce the sky…".

The New York skyscrapers and their tragic fate are the same continuation of the "conquest of the heavens": "up the down staircase".

In modern times the theme of the "ladder" loses its symbolic meaning but receives a new interpretation. The medieval opposition of earthly and heavenly is now projected onto a simultaneous perception: attic and cellar, paradise and the underworld, a staircase at once ascending and descending (H. Daumier, "The Laundress").

From early Christian times right up to the 21st century the eternal and unshakeable symbols remain Mount Sinai, where Moses received the tablets, Tabor, where Jesus Christ was transfigured before the disciples, and many other infinitely important symbols. And above them all is our chief and eternal symbol: the Cross with the Lord crucified on it, set up on the hill of Golgotha and giving us Salvation.
