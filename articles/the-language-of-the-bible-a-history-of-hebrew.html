The Language of the Bible

The Language of the Bible. A History of Hebrew

2017 · The Language of the Bible

For a long time Jews called Hebrew לשון קדש, the holy tongue.

A History of Hebrew
תּוֹלְדוֹת הַלָּשוֹן הָעִבְרִית

The name "Ivrit" actually means "Hebrew (language)". The name "Ivrit" is relatively new; it appeared about a hundred years ago, most likely as a calque of the European term "Hebraic", from the word עברי, Hebrew. Until then, for a long time, Jews called Hebrew לשון קדש, the holy tongue. In the Tanakh, in the book of Nehemiah, the language of the Jews is called יהודית, the Jews' language.
Hebrew belongs to the Semitic family of languages. Among modern languages, the Semitic group includes Arabic (the eastern and Maghrebi dialects), Aramaic (various dialects), Maltese (in effect a dialect of Arabic), Amharic (the state language of Ethiopia, which is also the language of most Ethiopian Jews), and various Ethiopian dialects.

4,000–3,000 years ago

In the view of the boldest Jewish and Christian theologians, it was in Hebrew that the Lord spoke with Adam in the Garden of Eden, almost 6,000 years ago. Scholars are more cautious in their estimates. But in the view of scholars too, Hebrew is an extremely ancient language.

From at least the 20th–21st centuries BC the land of Israel was called Canaan, כנען (Kna'an), and its inhabitants Canaanites, כנענים (kna'anim). To the north of Canaan lay the country later called Phoenicia; to all appearances the Phoenicians were the same Canaanites, who had stronger cities (Tyre, צור; Sidon, צידון; and others). As for language, apparently both the Phoenicians and all the Canaanites in general spoke practically the same language as the Jews. (When there was a need to translate from one language to another, the Tanakh mentions it; however, nowhere is there any mention of a need for interpreters when Jews dealt with Canaanites or with the inhabitants of Tyre, the Phoenicians.)

There is evidence of the Canaanite language from the 13th–14th centuries BC: the Tel el-Amarna cuneiform tablets. The tablets are letters from Canaan to Egypt, written in Akkadian (Assyro-Babylonian). However, here and there in the text, as comments, explanations and the like, words of the local (Canaanite) language are inserted, words that are used in Hebrew to this day: עפר, חומה, אניה, כלוב, שער, שדה, סוס, מס (see Abram Solomonik, "From the History of Hebrew"). Thus these words (then still in the language of the Canaanites) existed in practically their present form at least two hundred years before the conquest of Canaan by the Jews.
The biblical story of Abraham's journey from Ur to Canaan is confirmed by cuneiform tablets excavated in Iraq; but of course it is hard to say what language Jacob spoke with his sons, and what language the Jews spoke when they came out of Egyptian slavery. One thing is beyond doubt: the language we today call Hebrew is close to the language of the Canaanites, and perhaps is one of its branches. It is customary to regard Phoenician and Hebrew (and several other dialects) as members of the Canaanite family of languages (roughly as Russian and Ukrainian descend from Old Slavonic).
It should be noted here that in this era vowel sounds were not marked at all. The modern words מים, ארון, מלכים were written as מם, ארן, מלכם (L. Zeliger, "Hebrew"). So it is hard to judge when and how ancient Hebrew and Phoenician diverged, and how exactly they differed. The word שמים was still written by the Phoenicians later as שמם, but how can one know whether this difference was only in the writing, or whether the pronunciation was also very different?
The oldest surviving inscriptions in Hebrew found in Israel are almost 3,000 years old (the "Gezer calendar", in Russian). But scholars believe that the oldest texts of the Tanakh were composed even earlier, in the 12th century BC. This date is customarily regarded as the beginning of the history of the Hebrew language proper.

The ancient Hebrew (Phoenician) script. To all appearances the Jews took over their writing from the Canaanites. To all appearances it was the Canaanites who first began to use alphabetic writing. It is supposed that the Phoenician letters derive from Egyptian hieroglyphs. (The oldest variety of this script is called Proto-Canaanite.) From this alphabet derive almost all modern alphabets, including the modern Hebrew, Arabic, Greek and Latin. (Another alphabet, in which the shape of the letters was based on cuneiform, was used in the city of Ugarit to the north of Phoenicia, but this alphabet did not take root and disappeared with the destruction of the city.) Today the ancient Hebrew script (though in a greatly changed form) is used for writing the scrolls of their Torah by the Samaritans, a people that once split off from the Jews (today about 600–700 people).

2,500 years ago

After numerous contacts with Assyria and Babylonia, in particular after the destruction of the First Temple and the Babylonian Exile (about 2,500 years ago), Hebrew came under considerable influence of Aramaic. This showed both in borrowings (which later became established in the language) and in numerous turns of phrase, many of which later disappeared and survived only in literary monuments.
Interestingly, through Aramaic (more precisely, through the Babylonian version of Aramaic) not only purely Aramaic but also Sumerian (!) words entered Hebrew. (The Sumerians were the first inhabitants of Mesopotamia known to us, older than the Babylonians and Assyrians.) Thus the words היכל and תרנגול, which have taken root completely to this day, came into Hebrew from Aramaic, into Aramaic from Akkadian, and into Akkadian from Sumerian (see Baruch Podolsky, "Conversations about Hebrew"). The names of the months of the Jewish calendar also came from Babylon.

The modern form of the Hebrew letters also, to all appearances, came from Babylon: our script is called the "square" or "Assyrian" script. However, the ancient Hebrew (that is, Phoenician) script was also used by Jews right up to the Bar Kokhba revolt. The inscriptions on the coins struck by Bar Kokhba (in Russian) are the last inscriptions made in the ancient Hebrew script.
After the return of the Jews to the Land of Israel from the Babylonian captivity, a struggle for national revival began, including revival of the language. Nehemiah writes:

In those days also saw I Jews that had married wives of Ashdod, of Ammon, and of Moab: And their children spake half in the speech of Ashdod, and could not speak in the Jews' language, but according to the language of each people. And I contended with them (Neh. 13:23–25) גם בימים ההם ראיתי את היהודים השיבו נשים אשדודיות עמוניות מואביות: ובניהם חצי מדבר אשדודית ואינם מכירים לדבר יהודית וכלשון עם ועם: ואריב עמם

Besides this, many Jews had already switched to Aramaic in Babylon. The book of Ezra is written half in Aramaic; but the book of Nehemiah is already written entirely in Hebrew. The struggle for the national language was crowned with success. After the Babylonian captivity there were still Jews who knew Hebrew; despite the spread of Aramaic throughout the Middle East, the whole population of Judea again began to speak Hebrew, and went on speaking it for almost another thousand years.

The square (Assyrian) script. From Babylon the ancient Jews brought the letters we use now. In Jewish tradition these letters are called "Assyrian script", כתב אשורי (ktav ashuri), as opposed to the ancient script, כתב דעץ (ktav da'atz). The exact meaning of the word דעץ is not known; it is known only that the Talmud uses this word to describe the ancient Hebrew script. Nevertheless there is no doubt that the "Assyrian script" too developed from the Phoenician. (The modern Assyrian alphabet, on the other hand, is more like Arabic writing and only distantly resembles the Hebrew letters.)

2,000 years ago

After the destruction of the Second Temple and the Jews' loss of statehood, Hebrew gradually began to be displaced by Aramaic. As a result of two revolts against Rome (the Jewish War and the Bar Kokhba revolt), Judea acquired the reputation of a "rebellious province". The Romans continued repressions long after the last revolt had been drowned in blood, and the Jewish population of Judea steadily decreased. Jews fled to neighbouring countries, where the population spoke mainly Aramaic. At that time translations of the oldest Jewish literature into Aramaic were made (for example, Targum Onkelos). At the same time the code of Jewish law, the Mishnah, was written down. The Mishnah and the first commentaries on it were written in Hebrew; but the further it went, the more Hebrew was displaced by Aramaic. The Mishnah and the commentaries on it (the Gemara, the Tosefta) together make up the Talmud, the code of Jewish law (which exists in two versions, the Babylonian and the Jerusalem). If Hebrew was called leshon kodesh (the holy tongue), Jews began to call Aramaic leshon ha-hakhamim (the language of the sages), since most of the Talmud is written in Aramaic.
After the Arab conquest, following the grammarians of Arabic, the first attempts were made to analyse the grammar of Hebrew too: this was begun by Saadia Gaon (8th–9th centuries AD) and his pupil Menahem ben Saruq.

Vowel points. Hebrew finally ceased to be a living language around the 4th century AD. There arose a danger of losing the correct pronunciation of the sacred texts, and as early as the 6th century systems of vowel points were developed to fix the pronunciation. At first several systems of vowel points arose (the "Tiberian", the "Babylonian" and the "Palestinian"). By the 10th century the ben Asher dynasty of Tiberias had finally canonised a system of vowel points based on the Tiberian system, and this system became generally accepted. (The Babylonian system of vowel points is still used by Yemenite Jews to point some books.)
Full spelling. Gradually the "mothers of reading", the letters א, ה, ו, י, were introduced into the ancient spelling to mark some vowels. But at first the use of the "mothers of reading" was limited to certain grammatical phenomena, and in most cases depended only on the whim of the scribe. A little later, in the Talmudic era, the "mothers of reading" were already applied systematically.
