The Bible and the Artist

"How Beautifully You Sing": Why Study the Bible as Literature?

2015 · The Bible and the Artist

Author: Dmitry Slivnyak (source: ja-tora.com). Translated from Russian.

They say that when one of the biblical prophets called his listeners to repent, they answered him: "Ah, how beautifully you sing!" And they did not repent.

Opponents of literary approaches to the study of the Bible sometimes recall this story. Other popular arguments are "but it is sacred scripture", "but it is a religious text"… To be honest, such arguments used to throw me into some confusion. Though in fact there is no problem here. Sacred scripture: so what? A religious text: so what? And if the prophet said "repent", that referred to his contemporaries; people today have other sins. We will cope with our shortcomings somehow by ourselves, and the beautiful singing of the ancient prophet can still be listened to now. (In fact I myself do not find this answer very convincing; more on this below.)

As for sacred scripture, that is a status, not a genre. A religious community takes certain texts and declares them sacred. And literary merits, which help such texts to affect their audience, may play no small part in the choice of these texts. If a prophet sings badly, no one will listen to him at all. Finally, the Bible is a religious text in the sense that "the author calls on the reader to believe in God" (Ilya Ilf, Notebooks). But Dante calls for the same in the "Divine Comedy", Milton in "Paradise Lost" and Dostoevsky in "Crime and Punishment". No one denies that these texts belong to imaginative literature or that they can be analysed as literature.

With the Bible things were (and in part still are) different. For too long it was treated (and fundamentalists still treat it) as a source of indisputable commands, a kind of "textual sergeant-major". The Bible itself resists such treatment, and to derive instructions for every occasion from it one has to work hard (Jewish exegesis, for example, has been doing just that for more than two thousand years).

In modern times a historical approach to the study of the Bible appeared. It seeks to place the biblical texts in their political, ritual and cultural context and tries to reconstruct their history, sources and original form. This is undoubtedly good: the Bible ceases to be something abstractly absolute and gains concrete historical flesh and blood. Nevertheless, the sense of the unity and inner coherence of the text is often lost in the process. Literary analysis is meant, among other things, to restore this sense of coherence.

Let me give an example taken from Robert Alter's now classic monograph. Chapter 38 of Genesis tells the story of Yehudah and Tamar. This story breaks off the account of Yosef's adventures in the middle and at first sight has nothing to do with them. That is how "ordinary" biblical criticism treats the problem: for it the story of Yehudah and Tamar is "a completely independent unit, with no connection to the Joseph drama, which it interrupts at the end of the first act". With this approach it remains unclear why the editor needed to place it precisely here. The desire to slow down the action in the swiftly unfolding story of Yosef is obvious. But that is not all, and mainly it is not that. There are interesting echoes between the two stories. When the brothers bring Yaakov the garment of the torn Yosef, they say to him: "Recognise (haker-na) whether this is your son's garment or not" (37:32). On the other hand, in the next chapter Tamar says to Yehudah: "Recognise (haker-na) whose are these, the seal, the cord and the staff" (38:25). And just as Yaakov "recognised it" (Yosef's garment, va-yakirah), Yehudah "recognised" (va-yaker) his things, kept by Tamar (37:33 and 38:26 respectively). Finally, among the gifts Yehudah sent to Tamar was a kid (gedi izzim, 38:17), while Yosef's garment had been dipped in the blood of a kid (se'ir izzim, 37:31).

Other parallels between the stories: mourning his supposedly dead son, Yaakov says: "I will go down (ered) into the grave unto my son mourning" (37:35), while at the beginning of the next chapter Yehudah "went down" (va-yered; in the Russian translation used here, "went away") from his brothers (38:1), and when the story of Yosef resumes in chapter 39 we are told that he "was brought down" (hurad) to Egypt (in the Russian translation, "he was taken", 39:1). The "grave" into which Yaakov wants to go down to Yosef turns out to be Egypt, the "low place" to which he will indeed in time go down to meet his son. One more parallel: in chapter 37 Yaakov "refused to be comforted" (le-hitnachem, 37:35), while in the next chapter (38:12) Yehudah "was comforted" (va-yinnachem) after the death of his wife (in the Russian translation: "Yehudah's mourning ended"). In general, the motif of mourning on which the previous chapter ends runs through the whole of this story, in which Tamar too remains in widow's clothes for a time. From all this it follows that the story of Yehudah and Tamar is most closely connected with the preceding and following text and was not mechanically inserted into the story of Yosef.

Of course, such observations are not being made for the first time. Thus the midrash says: "The Holy One, blessed be He, said to Yehudah: You deceived your father with a kid. By your life, Tamar will deceive you with a kid (…). You said to your father 'recognise'. By your life, Tamar will say to you 'recognise'" (Bereshit Rabbah 84:11–12). Nevertheless, the approach of the traditional midrash differs from the modern literary approach. First, the midrash is bluntly didactic: see how bad it is to deceive one's father, and punishment is inevitable. Second (and this is perhaps more important), the midrashist "cannot see the wood for the trees": behind the contexts he compares he does not see the text as a whole and pays no attention to the fabric of the story, to the crossing of its threads and its narrative knots.

Literary analysis of the biblical text seeks to make up for this lack. The approach, which has been developing actively since the 1960s, has shown that biblical narrative prose is no less "literature" than biblical poetry, about which this has long been known, and in literary terms is in part more original than the poetry. It has been shown that behind the conciseness and apparent simplicity of the biblical stories lies a refined narrative technique; that the Bible is often ambiguous, ironic and sometimes simply sly; and that the meaning and the lesson of a story are inseparable from its form and are realised in it. So in the Bible "repent" is inseparable from "how beautifully you sing", and if one pays no attention to the beauty of the singing, the repentance does not come out right either.
