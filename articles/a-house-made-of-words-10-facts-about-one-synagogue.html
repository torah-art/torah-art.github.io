The Bible and the Artist

A House Made of Words: 10 Facts About One Synagogue

2019 · The Bible and the Artist, Art Midrash

The author of the New Synagogue in Mainz, the architect Manuel Herz, on the ideas behind modern synagogues.

Here is Manuel Herz's talk (in German with Russian subtitles):

Source: Maria Vul, booknik.ru. Translated from Russian.

On what principles modern synagogues are built, and what architects can learn from the Talmudists, was the subject of a lecture, "The Light of the Dispersion: Tradition and the Avant-Garde in Architecture", organised by the Eshkolot project together with the Goethe-Institut, by the author of the New Synagogue in Mainz, the architect Manuel Herz.

1. In the 18th century the building of synagogues flourished both in Germany and in Europe in general. Synagogues were then built in two styles, very different, but going back to one and the same argument. An example of one of the styles is the synagogue in Hanover, which hardly differs from a Christian church. Neo-Gothic suddenly reflecting religious space in the culture of Judaism: how about that? It was explained like this: the Jews had received civil rights and could at last build exactly like other citizens, including building houses of prayer exactly as other citizens build theirs. The other style, like the synagogue in Aschaffenburg, is the Middle Eastern tradition. But the argument is the same: the Jews had received civil rights and could now show their differences, keep their tradition, present their national self-awareness, show where they came from.

2. Neither is a traditional style for Judaism. Both are borrowings. But what, then, is the idea of space from the point of view of Judaism? Let us remember the exodus of the Jews from Egypt and the journey through the Sinai desert. There God gives the chosen people a commandment: He tells the people walking through the desert that on the Sabbath, the seventh day, they must stay in their home and not leave it. But where in the desert, a place of indefiniteness without boundaries or measurable space, can one find a home? The commandment prompts one to create a certain idea of home both in the desert and in any other alien space. So the problem comes down to a simple practical question: how, anywhere and with no building materials, can one create a place where one will feel at home?

3. The next important thing that gave impetus to all Jewish self-definition (not only architectural) was the destruction of the Temple and the loss of Jerusalem. That is the beginning of the history of the diaspora, the beginning of the dispersion of the Jewish people, of life in exile. Moreover, all the rituals were closely bound up with the Temple, so the religion needed rethinking. The question arose at once: how, in such a situation, can a sense of unity be created at all? Here is the most radical consequence of the loss of Jerusalem: instead of trying to recreate the Holy City somewhere else, to build a substitute for it, the rabbis of that time decided to write a Book. The Talmud, 10,000 pages written over several centuries and overgrown with commentaries, arose as a reaction to the beginning of the dispersion. In effect it was a code of rules regulating everyday life without the Temple. This book was to become (and, on the whole, did become) a kind of single space, a symbol of unity, an instrument by which the people could unite anew when the time for it came.

4. At the same time the Talmud is a book permeated with architectural thought, constructed like a building, according to architectural canons. It is made in a definite form and must be read in a definite way: the ancient text in the centre is surrounded by the text of commentaries on it, and the commentaries on the commentaries are a little further out, practically in the margins. Further still are commentaries on the commentaries on the commentaries, so that on every page you see before you 600 years of the making of the text. (Incidentally, the huge and majestic Cologne Cathedral, one of the German symbols of Christianity, took 700 years to build: quite comparable periods.) This very spatial layout resembles the plan of a building in section. And there is something else: the Talmud is a conversation of rabbis with one another across centuries, across time; it is a discussion carried on by people who lived at different times in different places. Simply, on the pages of this book they find themselves in one place at one time. No cathedral ever dreamed of anything like it.

5. The Talmud has a chapter devoted directly to space and its questions. It goes back to the commandment in which God ordered that one not leave one's place on the Sabbath day. What may one not do on Shabbat? For example, carry objects from private space into public space. How are private and public space defined? This is laid down very interestingly, cleverly and cunningly. Say, a space that is fenced, closed on four sides, is considered private; if it is fenced on only three, it is public, open. Take a bus stop: it is enclosed on three sides, but when a bus pulls up, the space is closed. That is, while the bus is standing at the stop, this space is considered private in Jewish law; when it pulls away, the space becomes public again. Not only a bus stop can be public and private at once; this holds true for a whole building, a district, a city. Suppose you take a book in your hands and it is the Sabbath around you: by the law you may carry it from place to place inside your house, but you may not carry it out into the street. For this to become permitted, the whole city must be closed, considered private space. And this can be done: say, by surrounding it with a thread, a wire or a cable, marking the boundary. If this boundary is set up with the observance of certain rituals, the whole city becomes your private space on Shabbat: carry the book wherever you like. The rules can be stretched very far: if, say, a thread is stretched across a gap in a wall, that is enough for the wall to be considered continuous. From an architect's point of view this is a beautiful and radical way of thinking: the means are minimal, but the effect of using them is enormous.

6. Another important lesson an architect can take from the Talmud is watching the mechanics of rabbinic discussion. The participants in the dispute use letters like bricks, to build new meanings. In the rabbinic tradition it is completely natural to rearrange the letters within a word (especially since Hebrew allows this easily) in order to get an anagram, a new entity, an elegant turn of the discussion, a new building of thought. The whole world is built of letters.

7. Mainz is one of the most important cities for European Jewry; its community stood at the origins of Ashkenazi Judaism, and in the 11th century famous and respected rabbis lived there (for example, Gershom ben Judah, called "the Light of the Exile"). Before the Holocaust there were three synagogues in the city; all were destroyed. In creating his design, Herz wanted to point to the importance of Mainz for Judaism and for the Jews, but not to touch the theme of National Socialism in the architecture. "I thought that in that case the Nazis would become co-authors of my project," he says. Besides this, a low building had to be fitted into a space surrounded by houses of six or seven storeys so that it would not give way to the urban context but stand out, not letting itself be overwhelmed.

8. The exact shape of the building in horizontal section refers to letters, to a word. It is the word קדושה, "kedushah", meaning "exaltation" or "blessing": all five of its letters, presented in abstract form. In general a synagogue is a secular building, but the way it is used makes it special, sacred, at the moment of use. The shape of the façade and the word encoded in it support the sacred status of the place. The façade is three-dimensional, with a ribbed ceramic surface; 15,000 pieces went into it. The arrangement of these elements, of this pattern, is such that it creates a multitude of perspectives. Herz's team, as he himself says, "explored the limits of the possible" in architecture: for example, they built out of concrete, at a height of 25 metres, a corner overhanging the street. The glazed ceramics with which the façade is faced reflect sunlight superbly and so give a wide palette of colours.

9. All the surfaces inside the prayer hall are a mosaic relief of millions of letters. Their surface is smooth, while the surface of the space between them is rough, which echoes the finish of the façade and also gives light and shade. The result is a shimmering, glimmering ocean of letters, which in a number of places form a text. Among other things, on the walls one can find a psalm in which the study of the Torah, love for the Bible, is compared metaphorically with the love of a man for a woman. A text about kisses and embraces is, frankly, rare in the interior decoration of synagogues, but in this one it does not look out of place at all.

10. There is not a single six-pointed star in the synagogue. "It seems to me this symbol is out of place here. Modern times have greatly changed its meaning. The synagogue in Mainz should refer to the history of Jewry in Mainz, not to the ancient Temple, not to the Holocaust and not to the State of Israel," Herz says. And he adds: "I am a Jew, I am an architect, but I am not a Jewish architect. I would not want to build only synagogues."
