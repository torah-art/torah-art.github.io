The Bible and the Artist

Still Life as an Image of the Eucharist

2015 · The Bible and the Artist

Author: Lilia Ratner. Translated from Russian and published with the author's permission.

The Bible is full of pointers to the beauty of creation: "Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin: And yet I say unto you, That even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. Wherefore, if God so clothe the grass of the field, which to day is, and to morrow is cast into the oven, shall he not much more clothe you, O ye of little faith?" (Matt. 6:28–30).

The description of the tabernacle, where the Ark of the Covenant was kept, given by God to Moses on Mount Sinai, is truly a catalogue of decorative art. Several pages of the book of Exodus are taken up with the most detailed descriptions of the Ark of the Covenant, the table for the showbread, the golden lampstand, the tabernacle, the veil, the bronze altar and so on (Exod. 25, 26, 27). The description of the Temple and of the royal palace is the same. The world of the Bible is full of objects. Man fell through an apple, and through bread and wine, changed into the Body and Blood of Christ, he gains salvation.

The consciousness of ancient people endowed the world of objects with human qualities, spiritualised the material and humanised the non-human. In fairy tales things come to life and are felt to be kind or predatory. The desire to materialise a miracle is natural to man, and especially to man the artist.

Still life is a genre in which things speak, communicate with one another and with us. From them we learn about the artist, their maker, and through this about his and our Maker. Encountering a work of art is always a dialogue between the author and the viewer. A work of art is interesting precisely because, with the artist's help, the subject opens its soul, inviting us to a confession in return. Art teaches people to perceive the world as a value, teaches them to see and understand the world, to love it, and therefore to communicate with it as with a friend. With colour, line and rhythm it can express what speech cannot. Like Pygmalion, who turned the statue he had carved into a living girl, the viewer can see in a painting not paint, stone or wood but living beings like himself. The characters of a still life can confess before us, letting us read their unspoken thoughts and feelings. In a still life a thing is more than itself, because the artist treats Being as a special value, and this is passed on to the viewer as he encounters the work.

Willem Kalf, Still Life with a Late Ming Ginger Jar

The rise of the Dutch still life cannot be considered except in the context of the spiritual movement of the 16th century that contributed to the crisis of Renaissance culture: in the context of Protestantism, and of the Calvinist kind as the most characteristic of Holland. The Reformation touched not only the church tradition. It was an upheaval in the whole life of Dutch society. "The individualism born of Protestantism drew attention to the subjective forms of the life of the person, of nature and of society" (A. F. Losev).

Calvin represented a strict, puritan, ascetic Protestantism. He acknowledged art as one of God's gifts; he knew the words of St Gregory about images as the Book of the illiterate. But in his writings and sermons Calvin stressed that the Spirit of God had judged otherwise: "The whole people should learn to read, and to come to know the Bible through images is frivolous and offensive to God. God is too exalted for man and the human eye. The artist's fantasy can only spoil His boundless perfection." Calvin's followers went further. Paintings on religious subjects were removed from the churches. Art was left a pedagogical task, the strengthening of morals; the role of painting was reduced at best to catechism. Art was seen as a secondary occupation not touching the essence of faith, since the Protestant service had no need of a sensuous setting. It was more for the ear than for the eye. At the same time faith embraces all of a person's activity. Religion sanctifies all of everyday life. And gradually art separated itself more and more from the Church. This hastened the development of secular tendencies in art. The painting moved from the church into the homes of the burghers. But God went on speaking in the language of art. It was this that made Dutch artists turn with astonishing constancy to the genre of the "stilleven", which means "still life"; this name reveals the essence of the genre far more truly than the word more familiar in Russian, "nature morte", "dead nature".

Pieter Claesz

In creating their "desserts", as they were sometimes called, the Dutch painters kept the composition almost unchanged from picture to picture. All the objects in them speak; all are symbolic. The first "stillevens" are simple: bread, a glass of wine and a fish, symbols of Jesus Christ; a knife, a symbol of sacrifice; a lemon, a symbol of unquenched thirst; a few nuts in their shells, the soul bound by sin; an apple recalls the Fall, and so on. Everything stands in good order, as if in solemn attendance.

There are still lifes in which the glass has fallen and the wine has spilled: an image of the world prevailing.

Gradually still lifes began to fill with earthly riches: carpet tablecloths, silver goblets, mother-of-pearl. Simple food is replaced by oysters, ham and exotic fruits. Painted with splendid materiality, such a canvas spoke of the vanity of human life, of the sinful love of earthly riches. So the Dutch "stilleven" became a memory of two feasts: the Last Supper, which gives life, and Belshazzar's feast, which leads to death. Such are the still lifes of Pieter Claesz (1597–1661), Willem Heda (1594–1680), Abraham van Beyeren (1620–1699) and Willem Kalf (1619–1693).

Antonio de Pereda, "Still Life with a Clock"

All the objects take part in a kind of action. Warm light pierces the gloom, playing in highlights on the surface of glass and metal like a reflection of the Kingdom of Heaven. The shimmer of reflections and the colours glowing from within speak of a peculiar, mysterious life, of the life of the private person sanctified by God. In some still lifes a clock is hidden, a reminder of the brevity of earthly life.

The artists of the 17th century are often called the "Little Dutch Masters". It is usually thought that this is because of the small size of their canvases and a certain narrowness of the burgher life they depicted. But the word more likely goes back to the Gospel: "thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes" (Matt. 11:25). God blesses simple life, its modest joys and sorrows. And so, although half-darkness surrounds the objects, a golden ray of light seeps in from somewhere and, like God's light, pierces the darkness of human life.

Frans Snyders

Unlike the Dutch, the Flemish still life astonishes with brilliance, variety and an abundance of every kind of food. The huge canvases of Frans Snyders (1579–1657), who worked in Rubens's studio, glorify earthly abundance: a variety of fruit, vegetables, game, fish, oysters, crabs and so on. The hunter's spoils: a doe, a boar, a hare, a swan… Everything is gathered into huge heaps. It is no accident that these canvases are called "shops". Everything is painted with sensual delight. One does not immediately realise that all this beauty has been killed. Here truly is "nature morte", "dead nature". God gave man the power to name, and therefore to keep, every living thing, and man has killed every living thing, bringing his destructive power into the harmony of the world. This theme also sounds in the work of Jacob Jordaens (1593–1678), one of the greatest artists of 17th-century Flanders. In his work Belshazzar's feast clearly triumphs: a hypertrophy of the material, a Rabelaisian power of fleshly being. At the same time the Flemish artists seem to erase the line between living and non-living nature, revealing the constant transformation of one into the other and making them equal.

So the still lifes of the 17th century are not a still life but a stern spiritual battle, in which good fights evil, darkness light, life death; in which the invisible soul of things is opened to the viewer's eye, and he is called to remember the "unseen warfare" in his own soul.

Later the development of the still life went the way of ever greater secularisation. The still life ceased to be sacred, ceased to be an icon. Culture moved further and further from the Church, its mother, and in the end, by the 20th century, ceased to be religiously inspired. The still life became first a game, then an expression of psychological problems, then a literary or plastic exercise.

In the 20th century many artists made the still life a sign of their materialist ideas. "Human grief is no more than the grief of an electric lamp" was a Futurist slogan. Pipes, metal, cardboard, newspapers, tin cans, bottles and so on: in a word, all the noise of modern civilisation with its rubbish heaps and dirt, and also with its intoxication with scientific and technical progress.

Morandi

But in the 20th century too there are artists who gave their work to a still life one wants to call a "stilleven". Such, for example, is the Italian artist Morandi, whose bottles and kitchen utensils are full of humane feeling and compassion for their owners. His still lifes are filled with living life. The objects in them are animated and kind. They seem to be solving some of our pressing problems for us. They seem to feel anxiety and care for lost humanity. A spiritual life glimmers in them, and a longing for lost sonship. Sometimes it seems that they are quietly praying for us.

In the 19th century secular painting in Russia also took on the role of a preacher of Christianity. A. Ivanov, Ge, Kramskoy, Polenov, Vrubel and Nesterov each in his own way addressed deep Christian tasks. But the still life interested them least of all. On the other hand, in the work of Tolstoy or Fedotov, Soroka or Venetsianov, artists who did not set themselves grand tasks, objects become a revelation.

I would like to end with Pushkin's well-known poem, which, with his usual genius, simply expresses what a thing, an object, can become in the poetic consciousness:

A flower, dried and without fragrance,
I see, forgotten in a book,
and now with a strange reverie
my soul is filled:
Where did it bloom? When? In what spring?
And did it bloom long? And who picked it?
A stranger's hand, or one I know?
And why was it laid here?
In memory of a tender meeting,
or of a fateful parting,
or of a solitary walk
in the quiet of the fields, in the shade of the woods?
And is he alive, and is she alive?
And where is their little corner now?
Or have they already withered
like this unknown flower?

This is a whole panorama, a whole life lived, drawn and called to life by the sight of a single flower. "Consider the lilies of the field…"
